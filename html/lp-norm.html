A norm measures the size, or length, of a vector. The Lp norm takes the absolute value of each component, raises it to the power p, adds them up, and then takes the p-th root to undo the power. The choice of p decides how strongly large components count: The two most used cases are written out below: with p = 1 the L1 norm simply adds up the absolute values, and with p = 2 the L2 norm is the ordinary straight-line length, the square root of the sum of squares. The larger p gets, the more the largest component dominates, until in the limit p → ∞ only the largest absolute value is left: the maximum norm.

Read aloud: the L p norm of x equals the sum from i equals one to n of the absolute value of x i to the p, all to the power one over p. The L1 norm of x equals the sum of the absolute values of x i. The L2 norm of x equals the square root of the sum of x i squared. The L infinity norm of x equals the maximum over i of the absolute value of x i.

max

Maximum over the components max⁡i\max_imaxi​. Pronounced: "the maximum over i of". Picks the largest of the nnn absolute values. The unit ball of the maximum norm is a square (a cube in three dimensions).

sum

Summation ∑i=1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}∑i=1n​. Pronounced: "the sum from i equals one to n". Runs over the nnn components of the vector, e.g. the coefficients β1,…,βp\beta_1, \dots, \beta_pβ1​,…,βp​ of a regression model.

abs

Absolute value of a component ∣xi∣|x_i|∣xi​∣. Pronounced: "the absolute value of x i". Makes negative components count as much as positive ones.

p

Exponent p≥1p \ge 1p≥1. Pronounced: "p". In the L2 row it is set to 2. Small ppp treats all components evenly; large ppp lets the biggest component dominate. The set of vectors with norm at most 1 (the unit ball) is a diamond for p=1p = 1p=1, a circle for p=2p = 2p=2, and a square for p=∞p = \inftyp=∞; the corners of the diamond are why the lasso produces exactly-zero coefficients. For p<1p < 1p<1 the formula is no longer a proper norm.

root

Outer exponent 1/p1/p1/p, the p-th root. Pronounced: "to the power one over p". Brings the result back to the scale of the components: doubling every component doubles the norm. Regularization penalties often drop this root (ridge uses ∥β∥22\lVert \beta \rVert_2^2∥β∥22​ rather than ∥β∥2\lVert \beta \rVert_2∥β∥2​), which changes the scale but not which vectors count as larger.
