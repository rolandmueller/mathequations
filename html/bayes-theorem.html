Bayes' Theorem

P(H∣E)=P(E∣H) P(H)P(E)\htmlClass{term-post}{\textcolor{#8b5cf6}{P(H \mid E)}} = \frac{\htmlClass{term-like}{\textcolor{#10b981}{P(E \mid H)}} \, \htmlClass{term-prior}{\textcolor{#ec4899}{P(H)}}}{\htmlClass{term-evid}{\textcolor{#3b82f6}{P(E)}}}P(H∣E)=P(E)P(E∣H)P(H)​

Bayes' theorem turns a conditional probability around. It gives the probability of a hypothesis after seeing evidence from the probability of that evidence if the hypothesis were true, the probability of the hypothesis beforehand, and the overall probability of the evidence. It is how to guess a hidden die from a partner's hint, and why a positive test for a rare disease often means you are still probably healthy.

Read aloud: the probability of H given E equals the probability of E given H times the probability of H, over the probability of E.

Definitions

post

Posterior P(H∣E)P(H \mid E)P(H∣E). Pronounced: "the probability of H given E". What to believe after the evidence. Mixing it up with P(E∣H)P(E \mid H)P(E∣H) is the base-rate fallacy: a test that is 99% sensitive does not mean a positive result is 99% likely to be correct. Worked example: a disease affects 1% of people; the test catches 99% of cases and falsely flags 5% of healthy people. Then P(sick∣+)=0.99⋅0.010.99⋅0.01+0.05⋅0.99≈17%P(\text{sick} \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \cdot 0.01}{0.99 \cdot 0.01 + 0.05 \cdot 0.99} \approx 17\%P(sick∣+)=0.99⋅0.01+0.05⋅0.990.99⋅0.01​≈17%. In confusion-matrix terms this posterior is the precision.

like

Likelihood P(E∣H)P(E \mid H)P(E∣H). Pronounced: "the probability of E given H". How well the hypothesis predicts the evidence. Dice example: if the hidden die shows a 6 (hypothesis), how likely is the hint "the total of both dice is at least 10"?

prior

Prior P(H)P(H)P(H). Pronounced: "the probability of H". The base rate before the evidence, e.g. 1/61/61/6 for any face of a fair die.

evid

Evidence P(E)P(E)P(E). Pronounced: "the probability of E". Usually computed with the law of total probability, summing over all hypotheses: P(E)=P(E∣H) P(H)+P(E∣not H) P(not H)P(E) = P(E \mid H)\,P(H) + P(E \mid \text{not } H)\,P(\text{not } H)P(E)=P(E∣H)P(H)+P(E∣not H)P(not H). It makes the posteriors of all hypotheses add up to 1.
