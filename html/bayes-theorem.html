Bayes' Theorem

P(H∣E)=P(E∣H) P(H)P(E)\htmlClass{term-post}{\textcolor{#8b5cf6}{P(H \mid E)}} = \frac{\htmlClass{term-like}{\textcolor{#10b981}{P(E \mid H)}} \, \htmlClass{term-prior}{\textcolor{#ec4899}{P(H)}}}{\htmlClass{term-evid}{\textcolor{#3b82f6}{P(E)}}}P(H∣E)=P(E)P(E∣H)P(H)​

Bayes' theorem turns a conditional probability around. It gives the probability of a hypothesis after seeing evidence from the probability of that evidence if the hypothesis were true, the probability of the hypothesis beforehand, and the overall probability of the evidence.

Running example, used in every definition: a disease affects 1% of people. A test detects 99% of sick people, but also wrongly flags 5% of healthy people. You test positive. The hypothesis H is "I have the disease", the evidence E is "my test is positive". Bayes' theorem shows why you are still probably healthy.

Read aloud: the probability of H given E equals the probability of E given H times the probability of H, over the probability of E.

Definitions

post

Posterior P(H∣E)P(H \mid E)P(H∣E). Pronounced: "the probability of H given E". What to believe after the evidence. In the example, the probability of having the disease given a positive test: P(sick∣+)=0.99⋅0.010.0594≈0.17P(\text{sick} \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \cdot 0.01}{0.0594} \approx 0.17P(sick∣+)=0.05940.99⋅0.01​≈0.17. Only about 17%, despite the 99% detection rate. In counts: of 10,000 people, 100 are sick and 99 of them test positive; of the 9,900 healthy people, 495 also test positive. So 99 of the 594 positive tests are real: 99/594≈17%99 / 594 \approx 17\%99/594≈17%. Mixing up P(H∣E)P(H \mid E)P(H∣E) with P(E∣H)P(E \mid H)P(E∣H) (17% vs. 99% here) is the base-rate fallacy. In confusion-matrix terms, this posterior is the precision of the test.

like

Likelihood P(E∣H)P(E \mid H)P(E∣H). Pronounced: "the probability of E given H". How well the hypothesis predicts the evidence. In the example, the probability of a positive test if you are sick: P(+∣sick)=0.99P(+ \mid \text{sick}) = 0.99P(+∣sick)=0.99, the test's sensitivity (recall). It describes the test, not you: it is the same in every population, whether the disease is rare or common.

prior

Prior P(H)P(H)P(H). Pronounced: "the probability of H". The base rate before the evidence. In the example, the share of people who have the disease: P(sick)=0.01P(\text{sick}) = 0.01P(sick)=0.01. It drives the result: in a group where 20% are sick, the same positive test would mean an 83% chance of being sick.

evid

Evidence P(E)P(E)P(E). Pronounced: "the probability of E". How often the evidence occurs at all, whether or not the hypothesis is true. In the example, the share of all people who test positive, computed with the law of total probability from sick and healthy people: P(+)=P(+∣sick) P(sick)+P(+∣healthy) P(healthy)=0.99⋅0.01+0.05⋅0.99=0.0594P(+) = P(+ \mid \text{sick})\,P(\text{sick}) + P(+ \mid \text{healthy})\,P(\text{healthy}) = 0.99 \cdot 0.01 + 0.05 \cdot 0.99 = 0.0594P(+)=P(+∣sick)P(sick)+P(+∣healthy)P(healthy)=0.99⋅0.01+0.05⋅0.99=0.0594. Most of these 5.94% are false alarms from the large healthy group, which is why the posterior is low. Dividing by P(E)P(E)P(E) makes the posteriors of all hypotheses add up to 1: P(sick∣+)≈0.17P(\text{sick} \mid +) \approx 0.17P(sick∣+)≈0.17 and P(healthy∣+)≈0.83P(\text{healthy} \mid +) \approx 0.83P(healthy∣+)≈0.83.
