Law of Total Probability

The overall probability of an event is often easiest to find case by case. Split the world into cases that cannot overlap and together cover everything, find the probability of the event within each case, weight it by how common that case is, and add up over all cases. With just two cases, H and not H, the sum has two terms: the event within the other case, weighted by that case's probability.

Running example, the same as on the Bayes' theorem page: a disease affects 1% of people, a test detects 99% of sick people and wrongly flags 5% of healthy people. How many people test positive overall? 0.99 · 0.01 + 0.05 · 0.99 = 0.0594, about 6%. This total is exactly the denominator of Bayes' theorem.

Read aloud: the probability of E equals the sum over all k of the probability of E given H k times the probability of H k. For two cases: the probability of E equals the probability of E given H times the probability of H, plus the probability of E given not H times the probability of not H.
